## 1. Why Read Fund Reports

The purpose of a fund report is not to predict next quarter's market move. It is to verify whether:

- the fund is still doing what it promised;
- risk exposure has changed;
- performance can be explained;
- fees and trading remain reasonable;
- management or holder structure has changed materially.

## 2. Start With the Fund's Mandate

From the fund contract, prospectus, or key information document, confirm:

- investment objective;
- eligible investments;
- benchmark;
- allocation constraints;
- derivatives, leverage, or cross-border exposure;
- major risks and fees.

Without knowing what the fund is supposed to do, it is impossible to judge whether actual behavior is consistent.

## 3. Compare Performance With the Benchmark

A fund's standalone return is incomplete information.

Ask:

- how it performed relative to its benchmark;
- whether the observation period is long enough;
- whether excess return is persistent;
- what volatility and drawdown were required to achieve it;
- for index funds, whether tracking difference is reasonable.

Short-term outperformance or underperformance does not automatically determine fund quality.

## 4. Manager Commentary

For active funds, commentary can help explain:

- how the manager interpreted the period;
- why the portfolio changed;
- whether the investment framework remains consistent;
- whether style drift is emerging.

But commentary is an explanation from the manager, not a substitute for holdings and realized results.

## 5. Portfolio Holdings

Review:

- stocks, bonds, and cash weights;
- sector concentration;
- top holdings and changes;
- duration and credit quality for fixed income;
- foreign-asset and currency exposure;
- whether actual exposure is consistent with the product name and mandate.

For index funds, compare the portfolio with the benchmark methodology as well.

## 6. Manager and Team Changes

A manager change may signal changes in process, style, or transition risk.

But an unchanged manager is not a quality guarantee. Long-run process, team structure, and results matter more.

## 7. Holder Structure

Institutional ownership, employee ownership, and large single holders can reveal behavioral and liquidity risks, but should not be interpreted mechanically.

A very large holder, for example, may create redemption pressure if it exits. Employee ownership may improve alignment, but does not guarantee future performance.

## 8. Fees and Turnover

Costs compound against investors. For active funds, also examine whether high turnover has produced durable value and whether trading behavior matches the stated strategy.

For index funds, fees and tracking difference deserve particular attention.

## 9. A Ten-Minute Reading Order

1. Objective and benchmark.
2. Latest asset allocation.
3. Top holdings and sectors.
4. Manager changes.
5. Performance versus benchmark.
6. Fees, size, and holder concentration.
7. Material events and risk disclosures.

The report is best used to test the investment thesis, not to mine the manager's outlook for short-term trading signals.

## 1. What a Bond Is

A bond is a debt claim created when an issuer borrows from investors. Typical cash flows include periodic interest and repayment of principal at maturity.

A bond is not automatically principal-protected. Receiving the promised cash flows depends on the issuer's ability to pay, the contract terms, and whether the investor holds the bond to maturity.

## 2. Price and Yield

For a conventional fixed-rate bond, market price and market interest rates generally move in opposite directions:

- when market rates rise, existing fixed coupons become less attractive and bond prices usually fall;
- when market rates fall, existing coupons become more attractive and prices usually rise.

Return cannot be evaluated from coupon rate alone. Purchase price, remaining maturity, cash flows, and embedded call features also matter.

## 3. Yield to Maturity

Yield to maturity is the internal rate that equates the present value of promised cash flows with the bond's current price under assumptions such as holding to maturity and receiving payments as scheduled.

It is useful for comparison, but it is not a guaranteed realized return because default, calls, reinvestment rates, and trading costs can differ from the assumptions.

## 4. Interest-Rate Risk and Duration

Longer maturity does not mechanically mean higher total risk, but for ordinary fixed-rate bonds, **higher duration generally means greater price sensitivity to interest-rate changes**.

This corrects the historical note's broad claim that short bonds are riskier than long bonds. Short-duration bonds usually have lower interest-rate sensitivity, although credit, liquidity, and structural risks can differ greatly across securities.

Duration is an approximation for relatively small rate moves; convexity becomes more relevant for larger changes.

## 5. Credit Risk

Credit risk is the possibility that an issuer fails to make interest or principal payments as promised.

Credit analysis can include:

- earnings and cash flow;
- leverage and debt-service capacity;
- maturity structure;
- collateral, guarantees, and covenants;
- industry and macroeconomic risks;
- ratings, while recognizing that ratings are not guarantees.

Sovereign, local-government, policy-bank, and corporate debt have different sources of credit risk and should not all be described as simply “risk-free.”

## 6. Other Major Risks

- **Reinvestment risk**: coupons or returned principal can only be reinvested at lower rates.
- **Liquidity risk**: selling may require a large discount or may be difficult.
- **Call risk**: the issuer may redeem the bond when it is advantageous to do so.
- **Inflation risk**: fixed nominal cash flows may lose purchasing power.
- **Currency risk**: exchange-rate changes affect home-currency returns on foreign bonds.

## 7. Bond Funds vs. Individual Bonds

An individual bond has a defined maturity date. An open-end bond fund normally does not have an equivalent “hold to maturity and receive par” mechanism at the fund level. Its NAV continually reflects portfolio duration, credit spreads, subscriptions, redemptions, and trading.

A bond fund should therefore not be treated as simply a basket of principal-guaranteed instruments.

## 8. The Role of Bonds in a Portfolio

Bonds can help:

- provide contractual cash flows;
- reduce some equity exposure;
- match future liabilities;
- diversify risk in some environments.

The result depends on duration, credit quality, currency, and market conditions. The first question should be whether the portfolio needs liquidity, stability, liability matching, or additional credit spread—not simply “which bond has the highest yield.”

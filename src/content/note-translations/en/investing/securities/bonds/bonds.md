## 1. What It Is
- An investment made to obtain a creditor claim: in simple terms, a government or company borrows money from investors and agrees when to repay principal and how much interest to pay.

## 2. Classification

### 2.1. Interest-Rate Bonds
Issued by central and local governments, policy banks, central banks, and similar institutions. They are generally associated with sovereign/public credit and tend to have very low credit risk; in simple terms, the risk of non-payment is relatively low.

They mainly include government bonds, policy-bank financial bonds, central-bank bills, and local-government bonds.

#### 2.1.1. Government Bonds
- In essence, the state borrows money from us.
- Uses
    - Understand the market risk-free interest rate: short-term government-bond yields are often used as a proxy for the risk-free rate.
    - Understand the style of national fiscal policy.
    - Understand market demand for safe assets.
- Classification
    - Certificate government bonds
        - Purchase channels: banks, counters, online banking.
        - Return: principal and interest paid at maturity.
        - Liquidity: can be redeemed early; interest treatment depends on the holding period.
            - ![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TDoct/images/master/1603035627_20201018174915467_27677.png)
    - Electronic savings government bonds
        - Purchase channels: banks, online banking.
        - Return: interest paid annually.
        - Liquidity: can be redeemed early, subject to product rules.
            - ![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TDoct/images/master/1603035629_20201018174927924_30349.png)
    - Book-entry government bonds
        - Purchase channels: bank or securities account.
    - Government-bond ETFs
- Risk: backed by sovereign credit in the original note's context.
- Return: historically often compared with bank term deposits of similar maturity.
- Factors affecting government-bond selling prices
    - Market interest rates
        - The higher bank deposit rates are, the lower government-bond prices tend to be.
        - Inverse relationship.
    - Yield level of government bonds in the secondary market
        - More competitive alternatives can lower the selling price.
    - Underwriters' successful-bid cost
        - Higher purchase cost for underwriters can mean a higher price.
    - Desired speed of fund recovery by underwriters
        - If underwriters want to recover funds faster, they may set a lower price.
    - Underwriting fee income
        - Fee arrangements affect underwriters' pricing incentives.
    - Other distribution costs
        - Higher distribution costs can affect the selling price.

#### 2.1.2. International Bonds
- What they are
    - Issued by a domestic issuer and denominated in a foreign currency in this note's description.
- Characteristics
    - Broad sources of funds and large issue sizes.
    - Exchange-rate risk.
    - May carry sovereign backing depending on the issuer.
    - Freely convertible currencies are commonly used as units of account.

#### 2.1.3. Financial Bonds
Bonds issued by banks and non-bank financial institutions.

### 2.2. Credit Bonds
Issued by entities other than governments, including ordinary enterprises, listed companies, local-government financing vehicles, banks, and other financial institutions.

Examples include enterprise bonds, corporate bonds, commercial paper, medium-term notes, detachable convertible bonds, asset-backed securities, and subordinated debt.

## 3. Bond Risks
These broadly correspond to the bond classifications above.

### 3.1. Interest-Rate Risk
Bond prices and market interest rates generally move in opposite directions. For example, if a bond yields 2.5% while an otherwise comparable bank deposit yields 3.0%, the deposit becomes relatively more attractive.

- Bank deposit rates: [Deposit Rate Table - Cngold](https://bank.cngold.org/yhckll/)
- Bank lending rates: [LPR - China Money](https://www.chinamoney.com.cn/chinese/bklpr/)

### 3.2. Credit Risk
The bond issuer may fail to repay its borrowing.

## 1. What Asset Allocation Is

Asset allocation is the process of dividing capital among assets such as stocks, bonds, and cash. It answers which risks to own before deciding which specific product to buy.

The historical note used a fixed 10% / 20% / 30% / 40% household allocation template. Such templates can prompt useful questions, but they are not universal allocation formulas.

## 2. Four Variables That Drive Allocation

### Time horizon

The closer a known spending date becomes, the less appropriate it is to depend on highly volatile assets.

### Risk capacity

This depends on income stability, cash reserves, liabilities, family obligations, and the importance of the goal.

### Risk tolerance

A portfolio that can theoretically survive a 50% drawdown is still unsuitable if the investor is likely to abandon it after a 20% decline.

### Liquidity needs

Emergency funds and short-term spending should not depend on assets that may need to be sold during unfavorable market conditions.

## 3. Roles of Major Asset Classes

- **Cash and highly liquid low-risk assets** support near-term spending.
- **Bonds** provide contractual cash flows and duration exposure, but still carry interest-rate and credit risk.
- **Stocks** provide ownership exposure to corporate earnings and long-run growth while accepting greater price volatility.
- **Other assets** should be included only when their expected return source, liquidity, and diversification role are understood.

## 4. Diversification Is Not Product Count

Effective diversification depends on underlying exposures.

For example:

- three funds concentrated in large technology companies can still be one concentrated bet;
- multiple broad indexes can have substantial overlap;
- equities and credit bonds may both fall during severe economic stress.

Look through wrappers to actual holdings, regions, sectors, duration, credit quality, and factor exposure.

## 5. What Periodic Investing Solves

Periodic investing can:

- convert recurring cash flow into investments automatically;
- reduce the psychological burden of choosing one entry date;
- create a disciplined saving and investing process.

It does not guarantee better returns than lump-sum investing, and it does not make an expensive asset safe. When capital is already available, lump-sum versus staged entry is a tradeoff among market exposure, opportunity cost, and behavioral comfort.

## 6. Rebalancing

Market moves cause portfolio weights to drift away from target allocation. Rebalancing restores the intended risk structure.

Common approaches include:

- checking at fixed intervals such as every six or twelve months;
- rebalancing when weights move beyond predetermined bands;
- using new cash flows to add to underweight assets before selling existing holdings.

The objective is to maintain risk exposure, not predict the next market move.

## 7. Why the “100 Minus Age” Rule Is Too Crude

Age is only a rough proxy for investment horizon. It does not capture:

- income stability;
- pension resources;
- liabilities and family obligations;
- actual goal dates;
- behavioral tolerance for drawdowns.

Strategic allocation should therefore start with goals and the balance sheet rather than a single age formula.

## 8. A Minimal Executable Framework

1. List goals and their dates.
2. Keep a separate liquidity reserve for near-term needs.
3. Set target ranges for stocks, bonds, and cash for long-term capital.
4. Implement them with low-cost, understandable instruments.
5. Rebalance infrequently.
6. Redesign strategic allocation only when goals, horizon, or risk capacity changes.

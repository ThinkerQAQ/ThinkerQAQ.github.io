## 1. What Relative Valuation Is

Relative valuation compares a company with peers, an industry, or its own history using price multiples.

Common metrics include:

- PE: market value / net income;
- PB: market value / book equity;
- PS: market value / revenue;
- EV/EBITDA;
- dividend yield;
- cash-flow-based multiples.

## 2. A Low Multiple Does Not Automatically Mean Undervalued

The historical note contained mechanical rules such as “lower PE means better value,” “lower PB means lower risk,” and “lower PS means more investment value.” These are not generally valid.

Low multiples can reflect:

- cyclically high earnings;
- deterioration in the business model;
- leverage or asset-quality problems;
- lower expected growth;
- poor earnings quality.

High multiples can reflect strong growth or economics, but can also simply reflect excessive optimism.

## 3. PE

`PE = Market Capitalization / Net Income`

PE is most useful when earnings are positive, reasonably stable, and comparable.

Distinguish between historical, trailing-twelve-month, and forward PE.

Major limitations include:

- one-off accounting items;
- cyclicality;
- negative earnings;
- differences in capital structure.

## 4. PB

`PB = Market Capitalization / Book Equity`

PB can be useful for financial or asset-heavy businesses where book assets remain economically meaningful.

But book value is not economic value. Intangible assets may be understated, while weak assets or low-return equity can make a low PB misleading.

PB is therefore more useful when combined with ROE, asset quality, and capital adequacy.

## 5. PS

`PS = Market Capitalization / Revenue`

PS can help when profit is negative or margins are still developing, but revenue alone is not value.

Two firms with the same revenue can deserve very different valuations because of margin, capital intensity, customer-acquisition cost, and cash-flow economics.

## 6. PEG

PEG attempts to combine PE with expected earnings growth, but it depends heavily on the growth forecast.

“PEG below 1 means cheap” and “PEG above 1 means expensive” are not universal rules. Growth duration, capital needs, risk, and earnings quality still matter.

## 7. Dividend Yield

`Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend per Share / Current Share Price`

A high yield may indicate stable mature cash flow, a sharply falling share price, a one-off distribution, or expectations that the dividend will not be sustained.

Sustainability matters more than a simple comparison with a risk-free rate.

## 8. Historical Percentiles

A statement such as “current PE is at the 20th percentile of the past ten years” only says that the multiple is low relative to its own history.

It does not prove undervaluation because the business, industry, interest-rate environment, or risk premium may have changed.

Fixed 30% / 70% percentile thresholds should not be used as mechanical trading signals.

## 9. Choosing Comparables

The hard part is not computing the ratio; it is choosing truly comparable companies.

Useful comparables should have reasonably similar:

- business models;
- revenue drivers;
- profitability and capital structures;
- growth stages;
- accounting treatment.

## 10. Better Uses

Relative valuation is most useful for:

1. rapid screening;
2. identifying market-implied expectations;
3. cross-checking intrinsic valuation;
4. judging whether changes in multiples are consistent with changes in fundamentals.

It should not be reduced to “buy the lowest multiple.”

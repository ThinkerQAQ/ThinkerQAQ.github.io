## 1. What Investing Is

Investing means giving up some certain consumption or liquidity today in exchange for uncertain future cash flows, asset value, or other economic benefits.

The core problem is therefore not “finding the highest-return asset,” but making tradeoffs under goals, time horizons, and risk constraints.

## 2. Define the Goal Before Choosing the Asset

Every investment decision should answer at least:

1. What goal is this money for?
2. When might the money be needed?
3. How much permanent loss and temporary drawdown can be tolerated?
4. How much liquidity is required?
5. What explicit and implicit costs are involved?

Without a goal and time horizon, asking which asset is “best” is usually not meaningful.

## 3. Risk and Return

Higher expected return generally comes with greater uncertainty, but taking more risk does not guarantee a higher realized return.

Risk can include:

- market risk;
- credit and default risk;
- interest-rate risk;
- liquidity risk;
- currency risk;
- concentration risk;
- operational, structural, and counterparty risk.

Volatility and maximum drawdown are useful metrics, but they do not describe every type of risk.

## 4. Risk Capacity vs. Risk Tolerance

These are different concepts:

- **risk capacity** is the objective ability to absorb losses, influenced by income stability, liabilities, time horizon, and cash needs;
- **risk tolerance** is the subjective willingness to live through volatility and losses.

A portfolio normally has to respect the tighter of the two constraints.

## 5. Diversification

Diversification does not simply mean owning more products. It means reducing dependence on a single source of risk.

Check whether:

- underlying holdings overlap heavily;
- exposure is concentrated in one country, sector, style, or factor;
- assets that appear different become highly correlated under stress.

Several funds with different names can still represent the same underlying bet.

## 6. Liquidity

Liquidity is the ability to turn an asset into cash when needed at a reasonable cost.

The shorter and more certain the spending horizon, the less suitable highly volatile or difficult-to-sell assets become.

A long horizon may improve the ability to tolerate volatility, but it does not make risk disappear.

## 7. Fees and Friction

Costs compound against the investor. They may include:

- management and custody fees;
- distribution or service fees;
- subscription, redemption, or trading fees;
- bid-ask spreads;
- portfolio turnover and transaction costs;
- taxes and cross-border friction.

Strategies should be compared after foreseeable costs, not only on gross returns.

## 8. A More Durable Framework

A useful order is:

`Goal → Time Horizon → Risk Capacity → Asset Allocation → Product Selection → Costs → Rebalancing`

Product selection comes after asset allocation. Fund codes, popular sectors, and short-term rankings should not replace that sequence.

## 1. Sharpness Is Not Only About Autofocus

Whether a photograph looks sharp depends on at least four things:

1. whether focus landed in the right place;
2. whether depth of field covers the required region;
3. whether the camera moved during the exposure;
4. whether the subject moved during the exposure.

A blurred picture therefore cannot automatically be blamed on autofocus.

## 2. Focus Plane and Depth of Field

A lens is strictly focused around a plane, while viewers perceive a front-to-back range of acceptable sharpness as depth of field.

Major factors include:

- aperture: wider apertures usually create shallower depth of field;
- camera-to-subject distance: closer focusing usually creates shallower depth of field;
- focal length and framing;
- sensor format and final viewing conditions.

“Longer focal length always means shallower depth of field” needs comparison conditions. At a fixed position and aperture, longer focal length changes magnification. If you move back to preserve framing, camera distance also changes the result.

## 3. Creating Stronger Background Blur

You can combine several methods:

- increase subject-to-background distance;
- move closer to the subject;
- use a wider aperture;
- use a longer focal length or greater subject magnification.

Do not open the lens fully just for blur. In portraits with eyes on different planes, groups of people, or macro work, excessively shallow depth can leave important details outside the focus zone.

## 4. Autofocus Modes

Names vary by brand, but the common concepts are:

- **Single AF (AF-S / One Shot)**: locks after focusing, useful for static subjects;
- **Continuous AF (AF-C / Servo)**: keeps updating focus, useful for moving subjects;
- **MF Manual Focus**: useful when a stable, repeatable focus position is more important, and often practical for stars, macro work, or difficult low-light scenes.

AF-area modes are not universally tied one-to-one to AF-S or AF-C. Available combinations depend on the camera.

## 5. Why Focus Can Fail

Common causes include:

- being closer than the lens's minimum focus distance;
- aiming at an area with too little texture or contrast;
- insufficient light;
- subject motion beyond tracking capability;
- selecting the wrong target or AF area;
- depth of field being so shallow that a critical feature sits outside it even when focus is technically correct.

## 6. Shutter Time and Motion

Shutter time determines whether motion is rendered more like an instant or a path.

- Fast shutter speeds can freeze motion.
- Slow shutter speeds can record water, light trails, clouds, or people as movement.
- Panning follows the subject during exposure so the subject can remain relatively sharp while the background becomes directionally blurred.

There is no universal “moving subject = 1/1000 s” rule. Required shutter time depends on speed, distance, focal length, and the amount of blur you want.

## 7. Stabilization Cannot Freeze a Subject

Lens or in-body stabilization primarily compensates for **camera movement**. It cannot stop the subject from moving.

At night, stabilization may let you handhold at 1/10 s, but a person can still blur because they moved during that exposure.

The old “minimum shutter = 1 / focal length” rule is only a starting heuristic. High-resolution sensors, subject movement, stabilization capability, and shooting technique all change the practical limit.

## 8. Small Apertures and Diffraction

Stopping down increases depth of field, but very small apertures reduce fine-detail contrast through diffraction.

Landscape photography should not mechanically default to f/16 or f/22. Select aperture from depth-of-field needs, lens behavior, and output size. When a single frame cannot cover the needed depth, consider focus stacking.

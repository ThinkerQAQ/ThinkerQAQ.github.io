## 1. Reading Light Matters More Than Memorizing Settings

Light can be observed through four useful dimensions:

- **intensity**: how much light there is;
- **direction**: where the light comes from;
- **hardness or softness**: how abrupt the shadow edges are;
- **color**: whether the source is warm, cool, or mixed with other sources.

These concepts transfer between cameras and scenes far better than memorizing parameter recipes.

## 2. Direction of Light

### Front Light

Light comes roughly from the camera direction. Illumination is even and contrast is often lower, but shape may appear flatter.

### Side Light

Light comes from the side and builds form through tonal transitions, revealing texture and volume.

### Backlight

The light source is behind the subject. Backlight is not “bad light” that should generally be avoided. It can create:

- rim light;
- glowing translucent materials;
- sunrise and sunset effects;
- atmospheric portraits;
- visible haze and depth.

The challenge is managing dynamic range, flare, and a dark front side of the subject.

### Top and Bottom Light

Top light can produce strong shadows under eyes and noses. Bottom light creates an unusual shadow direction. Either can be used deliberately as a style.

## 3. Soft and Hard Light

The larger a light source appears relative to the subject, the softer it usually becomes. A relatively small source creates harder-edged shadows.

Clouds, curtains, and diffusion material can enlarge or diffuse the effective source. Direct sun on a clear day is a classic hard source.

“Overcast days are bad for photography” is not a useful rule. Overcast sky often acts as a huge soft source with lower contrast; it simply provides less directional light than direct sun.

## 4. Color Temperature: Source Physics vs. Camera Setting

Physically, higher-color-temperature light sources tend to appear bluer, while lower-color-temperature sources appear more orange or red.

But when you raise the **white-balance Kelvin setting** in a camera or Lightroom, you are telling the system that the scene light is bluer and needs more warm compensation. The rendered image therefore usually becomes warmer.

Both statements can be true:

> A higher-temperature light source is bluer, while a higher white-balance setting usually renders the photo warmer.

## 5. White Balance

White balance does not have to force every scene into perfect neutrality. It determines how the photograph interprets the color of the light.

Common approaches include:

- use a neutral reference or reliable preset when color accuracy matters;
- lock white balance under stable light for consistency across a sequence;
- use Auto WB when conditions change rapidly;
- deliberately preserve warm sunset or tungsten atmosphere when that color is part of the scene.

## 6. RAW and White Balance

Raw files preserve data closer to the original sensor capture, so white balance can normally be reinterpreted much more freely than with JPEG.

It is still too strong to say that “raw has no white balance.” The capture setting is stored as metadata, affects camera previews, and can be used by raw software as the initial interpretation.

## 7. Mixed Light

When daylight, indoor lamps, and neon exist in the same frame, one global white balance cannot make every region perfectly neutral.

Options include:

- changing or turning off one source;
- gelling artificial light to match the environment;
- accepting color differences as scene information;
- using local masks in post-processing for different regions.

Color consistency starts as a lighting problem before it becomes a slider problem.

## 1. Photography Is First a Process of Selection

Photography does not record everything in front of the camera. It selects a finite frame from the real world.

Every photograph contains at least five decisions:

1. **What to photograph**: what is the subject and theme?
2. **What to exclude**: which elements interfere with the message?
3. **Where to photograph from**: camera position determines perspective and spatial relationships.
4. **When to photograph**: pose, traffic, clouds, light, and the environment all change with time.
5. **How to render it**: exposure, depth of field, motion blur, color, and post-processing shape the final appearance.

Technical settings exist to serve these decisions, not replace them.

## 2. Framing

Framing answers a simple question: what deserves to exist inside the picture?

For each element, ask:

- Does it help explain the subject?
- Does it provide scale, space, or environmental context?
- Is it only visual noise?

Many photographs fail not because something is missing, but because irrelevant elements were never excluded.

## 3. Timing

A photograph records a slice of time. From the same position, body language, cars, clouds, tides, lights, and the height of the sun can completely change the structure of the frame.

Waiting, anticipation, and burst shooting therefore have value. Burst mode should increase the chance of catching a brief state; it should not replace observation.

## 4. Viewpoint and Camera Position

When the camera moves, the relative size of foreground and background objects changes. This is the core source of perspective change.

A low viewpoint can emphasize foreground scale and height. A high viewpoint can simplify overlaps. Moving closer exaggerates size differences between near and far objects; moving farther away makes those relationships flatter.

Focal length mainly changes angle of view and magnification. Camera position determines perspective. In practice photographers often change both together, which is why the concepts are easily confused.

## 5. Light

Light can be understood through at least four dimensions:

- intensity;
- direction;
- hardness or softness;
- color.

There is no single “correct” type of light. Backlight, sidelight, front light, and top light create different forms, contrast, and mood.

## 6. Where Technical Settings Belong

Exposure, focus, focal length, and post-processing are expressive tools.

A more durable order of operations is:

`Theme → Framing → Camera Position → Timing → Light → Exposure/Depth/Motion → Post-processing`

Decide what the picture should communicate before deciding which settings to use.

These Photography Notes come from historical learning records, but the public edition keeps only reusable photography knowledge.

Camera-menu details, phone-model specifics, fixed parameter recipes, and instructor-specific rules are not presented as universal principles when they do not generalize across devices and scenes.

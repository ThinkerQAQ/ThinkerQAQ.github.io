## 1. A Portrait Is First a Relationship Between Person and Environment

Portrait photography is not simply “wide aperture plus blurred background.” It requires simultaneous control of:

- expression and pose;
- the direction and quality of light on the face;
- perspective created by camera distance;
- whether the background supports the person;
- whether depth of field covers critical features.

When the environment carries useful information, it should not automatically be erased into blur.

## 2. Camera Distance Comes Before “85 mm Is the Portrait Lens”

Close camera positions exaggerate features nearer the lens, such as the nose or forehead. Moving farther away makes facial proportions appear flatter.

Focal length helps frame the subject from the desired distance:

- environmental portraits often use a wider field of view;
- half-body and close portraits often use standard to moderate telephoto focal lengths;
- very long lenses increase physical distance between photographer and subject, which can affect communication.

There is no universal mapping from face shape to focal length.

## 3. Wide Aperture Is Not the Default Answer

A wide aperture creates shallow depth and lets in more light, but it can also cause:

- one eye to be sharp while the other is outside the depth of field;
- rows of people in a group to fall out of focus;
- stronger lens aberrations at maximum aperture.

Choose aperture from depth-of-field needs. When the background is already clean, retaining more environmental detail may be more informative.

## 4. Front, Side, and Back Light

### Front Light

The face is evenly lit and exposure is easy to control, though form may appear flatter.

### Side Light

Creates tonal transitions and stronger facial structure. Turning the person's head can be more effective than constantly changing camera settings.

### Backlight

Can create rim light and atmosphere. The main problem is the brightness difference between face and background.

Options include:

- fill with a reflector, wall, or flash;
- move the subject so the background becomes darker;
- allow some unimportant background highlights to clip;
- preserve highlights in raw and lift the face later when the file supports it.

## 5. Window Light

A window acts like a large light source. Moving a subject closer usually increases intensity; a thin curtain can soften the light.

Overcast weather can work very well for window portraits because the sky becomes a huge soft source. Whether the result works depends on direction, background, and the person—not on a “sunny vs. cloudy” label.

## 6. High-Key and Low-Key Portraits

High key is not simply “increase exposure,” and low key is not simply “decrease exposure.”

- **High key** uses predominantly bright tones while keeping important structure.
- **Low key** uses predominantly dark tones and relies on selected highlights to define subject and shape.

They are tonal designs, not fixed EV values.

## 7. Focus

Single AF works for still subjects; continuous AF is preferable when the person moves. Face/eye detection can reduce the burden of manual point selection, but you still need to verify that the camera selected the intended person and eye.

For important shots, inspect the eyes at high playback magnification instead of trusting a small thumbnail.

## 8. Night Portraits

Night portraits contain two separate exposure problems:

- is the person sharp?
- does the environment retain its night atmosphere?

A very fast shutter plus strong flash may freeze the subject but turn the background black. Slower sync or a higher ISO can retain more ambient light. The shutter still needs to respect subject motion; stabilization alone cannot solve it.

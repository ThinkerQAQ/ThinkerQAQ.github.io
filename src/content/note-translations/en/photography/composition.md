## 1. What Composition Solves

Composition is not about forcing a subject into a template. It organizes:

- what the primary visual subject is;
- where the viewer enters the frame and how the eye moves;
- how foreground, middle ground, and background create space;
- whether the edges contain distractions;
- whether negative space and information density support the theme.

Good composition is first an information-design problem.

## 2. Start with Subtraction

Before pressing the shutter, inspect all four edges:

- Is there half a sign, branch, or person entering the frame?
- Does a tiny bright object steal attention?
- Do background lines intersect the subject awkwardly?
- Would moving half a step simplify the background?

The most effective compositional action is often not adding something, but moving, waiting, or excluding.

## 3. Horizontal and Vertical Frames

Horizontal frames naturally support lateral spread, environmental relationships, and stability. Vertical frames more easily emphasize height, depth, and top-to-bottom relationships.

These are not genre restrictions. Landscapes can be vertical and portraits can be horizontal. The dominant structure should determine orientation.

## 4. Common Composition Tools

### Centering and Symmetry

Useful for stability, order, frontal presentation, and geometric symmetry.

### Rule of Thirds

Placing subjects or major lines near thirds can quickly avoid putting every element in the center. The four intersection points are not mandatory targets.

### Repetition and Rhythm

Repeated windows, pillars, people, or textures create rhythm. The element that breaks the repetition can naturally become the subject.

### Triangles and Diagonals

Triangular structures often feel stable. Diagonals create motion and direction.

### Leading Lines

Roads, railings, shadows, rivers, and architecture can guide the eye. A leading line is most useful when it leads toward something meaningful rather than simply carrying attention out of the frame.

## 5. Foreground Is Optional

Landscape photography often uses foreground to build depth, but “always find foreground” can create meaningless rocks, flowers, or railings.

A foreground is useful when it adds:

- scale;
- depth;
- a visual entry point;
- a color or shape relationship with the subject.

If it contributes nothing, negative space may work better.

## 6. Camera Position Before Cropping

Cropping can improve edges and proportions, but it cannot change the perspective relationship established at capture.

Moving the camera forward, backward, sideways, up, or down can simultaneously change:

- subject/background overlap;
- line direction;
- relative apparent size of near and far objects;
- spatial layering.

This is why moving for composition matters more than standing still and relying only on zoom, although zoom remains a precise angle-of-view tool.

## 7. Composition Rules Are Not a Scorecard

A photograph is not automatically better because it follows the rule of thirds, nor automatically wrong because the subject is centered.

A more useful question is:

> Does every important position in the frame help the viewer understand what I want to communicate?

Composition methods are simply tools for answering that question.

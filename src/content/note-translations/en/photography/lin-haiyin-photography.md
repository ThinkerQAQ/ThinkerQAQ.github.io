# Lin Haiyin Photography Notes

## Exposure Triangle

Aperture:

Shutter speed: historical examples included 1/125, 1/300, and 1/1250.

ISO:

There is no universal exposure-parameter priority such as "aperture > shutter > ISO in darkness" or "ISO > shutter > aperture in bright light."

A more reliable approach is:

1. Choose aperture according to depth-of-field intent.
2. Choose shutter speed according to motion and camera shake.
3. Raise or lower ISO within the image-quality range you can accept.

## Camera Modes

**Aperture priority:** choose aperture; the camera chooses shutter speed. ISO may be fixed or automatic. Use exposure compensation when the metered result should be brighter or darker.

**Shutter priority:** choose shutter speed; the camera chooses aperture. ISO may be fixed or automatic.

For a warm/retro starting point, raising the camera's Kelvin white-balance value generally warms the rendered image, but "retro" is a broader color/tone style rather than a single WB setting.

## Focal Length

1. **Field of view**

With camera position unchanged, a longer focal length gives a narrower field of view and greater magnification.

Background blur also depends on aperture, focus distance, background distance, sensor size, and final composition; it is not determined by focal length alone.

2. **Cropping a wide-angle image**

Cropping a wide-angle image can reproduce a narrower field of view, but it throws away pixels and does not create additional distant detail.

If the camera position is the same, the cropped image keeps the same perspective relationships.

3. **Perspective at similar subject size**

When the subject is kept at roughly the same size in the frame, using different focal lengths usually means changing camera distance.

The camera position is what changes perspective:

- close camera + wider lens -> stronger near/far size differences;
- farther camera + longer lens -> smaller relative size differences between foreground and background.

The familiar "telephoto compression" look mainly comes from moving farther away to maintain the same composition.

4. **Rendering People With Different Focal Lengths**

The main practical question is not "which focal length makes a face type look good," but "what camera distance gives the facial perspective and subject-background relationship I want?"

5. **Natural Viewing Impression**

35–50 mm on full frame is often described as giving a relatively natural viewing impression, but "equivalent to the human eye" is an oversimplification. Human vision cannot be represented by one focal length.

6. **Telephoto Blur**

A longer focal length combined with a wide aperture, suitable shooting distance, and a distant background often gives strong background separation. 135 mm is one classic portrait option, not a formula.

## Depth of Field

Depth of field is influenced by:

- aperture;
- focusing distance;
- focal length;
- sensor format;
- final display size/viewing conditions.

Stopping down and focusing farther away generally increase depth of field. Focal length must be interpreted together with whether the framing is being held constant.

A cluttered background may benefit from shallow depth of field to simplify the image. A background with useful context may deserve deeper focus. This is a compositional choice, not a hard rule.

## 1. Landscape Photography Starts Before You Arrive

Many of the most important variables in landscape, city, and night photography are not camera settings:

- sun and moon direction;
- weather and cloud cover;
- tide, water level, and fog;
- building-light schedules;
- crowds and traffic;
- whether a viewpoint is accessible.

Technical settings can only handle the scene that exists. They cannot replace timing and location.

## 2. Establish the Visual Structure First

A practical on-location sequence is:

`Subject → Camera Position → Foreground/Background → Frame Edges → Focal Length → Exposure`

Move first to establish spatial relationships, then use focal length for precise framing.

Architecture also requires awareness of camera tilt. Tilting upward to fit a tall building creates converging verticals. You can accept that perspective intentionally or manage it with camera position, a shift lens, or perspective correction in post.

## 3. There Is No Fixed Landscape Exposure

For a static scene, a common priority is:

1. choose an aperture that satisfies depth-of-field and lens-performance needs;
2. use a lower ISO when motion does not need to be frozen;
3. let shutter time satisfy the remaining exposure requirement.

But once leaves, waves, people, cars, or stars move through the frame, shutter time becomes a creative variable rather than an arbitrary remainder.

## 4. Long Exposure

Long exposures can:

- smooth water;
- stretch vehicle lights into trails;
- record moving clouds;
- accumulate more light at night.

They can also introduce:

- subject-motion blur;
- more visible hot pixels or fixed-pattern noise;
- greater sensitivity to tripod movement, ground vibration, and wind.

Some cameras perform long-exposure noise reduction by recording a dark frame, so the camera may need roughly another exposure-length period after the shot. Whether to use it depends on shooting pace and post-processing workflow.

## 5. High Contrast and Dynamic Range

Sunrise, sunset, window views, and city night scenes often contain very bright and very dark regions at the same time.

A useful order is:

1. decide which highlights matter;
2. inspect the histogram or highlight warnings;
3. use a single raw frame when its dynamic range is sufficient;
4. use exposure bracketing and HDR when the scene exceeds what one frame can retain.

HDR is not about making every region equally bright. It is about extending usable tonal information.

## 6. Focus and Front-to-Back Sharpness

If an important foreground element is very close while mountains or buildings must also remain sharp, one frame may not provide enough depth of field.

Options include:

- changing camera position to reduce the extreme near foreground;
- selecting a reasonable smaller aperture;
- placing focus where the required depth can be covered;
- shooting a focus-shift sequence and blending it later.

Do not automatically choose the smallest possible aperture just to make everything from your feet to infinity sharp.

## 7. Tripods and Stabilization

A tripod stabilizes the camera; it does not freeze a moving subject.

Different bodies and lenses behave differently with stabilization on a tripod. “Always turn stabilization off on a tripod” should not be treated as a universal rule. Follow the specific equipment guidance and test long exposures.

Also watch for:

- remote release or self-timer use;
- center-column and leg stability;
- camera straps moving in the wind;
- bridge, boardwalk, or traffic vibration.

## 8. Night Photography Should Still Look Like Night

The goal of night photography is not necessarily to turn night into daytime.

Streetlights and signs may contain local bright areas, while important building detail, sky color, and faces should be preserved according to the intended image.

Exposure should support the feeling of night rather than merely filling the histogram evenly.

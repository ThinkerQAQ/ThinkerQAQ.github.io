## 1. What GDP Measures

Gross domestic product measures the value of final goods and services produced **within an economy** during a given period.

“Final” matters because the value of intermediate inputs is already embedded in final output. Counting them again would double-count production.

## 2. Three Perspectives

The same economic activity can be recorded from three perspectives:

- **production approach**: the sum of value added across industries;
- **expenditure approach**: the sum of final expenditures;
- **income approach**: the labor compensation, profits, and other income generated by production.

In principle, all three describe the same GDP through different accounting routes.

## 3. Expenditure GDP

A common identity is:

`Y = C + I + G + NX`

where:

- `C`: household consumption;
- `I`: investment, including fixed capital formation and inventory change;
- `G`: government consumption and investment in final goods and services;
- `NX`: net exports, exports minus imports.

Government transfer payments are not themselves current production and therefore are not directly counted as `G`. Spending by transfer recipients can later appear in other GDP components.

## 4. Nominal and Real GDP

Nominal GDP changes with both output and prices. Real GDP attempts to remove price changes and is therefore more useful for comparing actual production across time.

The GDP deflator can be written as:

`GDP Deflator = Nominal GDP / Real GDP × 100`

Discussions of economic growth therefore usually focus on real GDP or real GDP per capita rather than nominal totals alone.

## 5. GDP and National Income Concepts

GDP is organized around **where production occurs**. Gross national income, or GNI, is organized around **income received by resident units**.

The historical note used a simplified older GNP expression. Modern international statistics more commonly use GNI, so this edition avoids the misleading shorthand of “income of nationals abroad minus income of foreigners at home.”

## 6. GDP Is Not Welfare

GDP is important but is not a complete measure of wealth or quality of life. It does not directly answer:

- how income is distributed;
- the value of unpaid household work;
- environmental costs;
- leisure, health, or safety;
- whether balance sheets are healthy.

The right conclusion is not that GDP is useless, but that it measures production and value added rather than total welfare.

## 7. Long-Run Growth

Long-run improvements in living standards depend on sustained increases in productive capacity per person. Common sources include:

- capital accumulation;
- human capital;
- technological progress;
- better organization and institutions;
- total factor productivity growth.

Increasing inputs can raise total output, but sustained growth in output per person usually depends heavily on productivity.

## 8. Other Indicators

GDP should be read alongside indicators such as employment, inflation, PMI, industrial production, credit, consumption, investment, and trade.

One monthly indicator rarely justifies a strong causal conclusion about the entire economy.

## 1. Demand

Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices over a given period.

Holding other factors constant, a higher price usually corresponds to a lower quantity demanded, while a lower price corresponds to a higher quantity demanded. This is a movement along the demand curve.

Factors that can shift the entire demand curve include:

- income;
- preferences;
- prices of substitutes and complements;
- expectations about the future;
- the number of consumers.

It is important to distinguish:

- **a change in quantity demanded**: caused by the good's own price, moving along the curve;
- **a change in demand**: caused by another factor, shifting the curve.

## 2. Supply

Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to provide at different prices over a given period.

Holding other factors constant, a higher price usually corresponds to a higher quantity supplied. Common supply shifters include:

- input costs;
- technology;
- taxes and subsidies;
- the number of producers;
- expectations about future prices and business conditions.

The same distinction applies between a change in quantity supplied and a shift in supply.

## 3. Market Equilibrium

The intersection of supply and demand determines an equilibrium price and quantity in the basic model.

- A price above equilibrium usually creates excess supply.
- A price below equilibrium usually creates excess demand.
- Price changes create incentives for buyers and sellers to adjust quantities.

## 4. Direction of Curve Shifts

For one curve shifting at a time:

- demand increases → price rises, quantity rises;
- demand decreases → price falls, quantity falls;
- supply increases → price falls, quantity rises;
- supply decreases → price rises, quantity falls.

When both curves move, one outcome may be ambiguous:

- **supply increases + demand decreases** → price falls, quantity is ambiguous;
- **supply decreases + demand increases** → price rises, quantity is ambiguous;
- **supply increases + demand increases** → quantity rises, price is ambiguous;
- **supply decreases + demand decreases** → quantity falls, price is ambiguous.

The historical note had the price direction reversed in the first two simultaneous-shift cases; those errors are corrected here.

## 5. Price Floors and Ceilings

A binding price floor above equilibrium can create excess supply. A binding price ceiling below equilibrium can create shortages.

Real outcomes also depend on enforcement, rationing, market structure, and how participants can adapt, so a supply-demand diagram does not capture every institutional consequence.

## 6. Taxes and Subsidies

A per-unit tax creates a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers receive and generally reduces the quantity traded. Who ultimately bears the tax depends mainly on the relative elasticities of supply and demand, not on who is legally required to send the tax payment.

A subsidy typically creates a wedge in the opposite direction and may increase the quantity traded. Welfare analysis still depends on elasticities and on whether externalities or other market failures are present.

## 1. What Elasticity Measures

Elasticity measures the percentage response of one variable to a one-percent change in another. Because it uses relative changes, it is more useful than raw slope for comparing different goods and units.

## 2. Price Elasticity of Demand

Price elasticity of demand can be written as:

`Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price`

Demand curves usually slope downward, so the elasticity is usually negative. In practice, discussions often compare the absolute value:

- `|Ed| > 1`: elastic demand;
- `|Ed| = 1`: unit elastic;
- `|Ed| < 1`: inelastic demand.

More available substitutes, more time to adjust, and a larger share of the consumer's budget generally make demand more elastic.

## 3. Arc and Point Elasticity

For a relatively large price change, the midpoint method can be used to calculate arc elasticity and reduce dependence on the choice of base point.

Point elasticity is more appropriate for a local change around a particular point on a demand curve.

## 4. Elasticity and Revenue

Total revenue is:

`TR = P × Q`

Therefore the effect of a price change on revenue depends on demand elasticity:

- with elastic demand, a price cut may increase revenue because quantity rises proportionally more;
- with inelastic demand, a price increase may increase revenue because quantity falls proportionally less;
- near unit elasticity, the two effects roughly offset each other.

This does not create a universal rule such as “elastic → charge a low price” or “inelastic → charge a high price.” Costs, competition, long-run demand, regulation, and strategy also matter.

## 5. Income Elasticity

Income elasticity measures how demand changes when income changes:

- positive income elasticity usually indicates a normal good;
- negative income elasticity usually indicates an inferior good;
- among normal goods, a higher income elasticity means demand is more sensitive to income growth.

These classifications can vary across consumers, periods, and markets.

## 6. Cross-Price Elasticity

Cross-price elasticity measures how the price of one good affects demand for another:

- positive: usually substitutes;
- negative: usually complements;
- near zero: weak relationship.

It can help analyze competitive relationships, but the relationship should be estimated from evidence rather than inferred only from intuition.

## 7. Supply Elasticity

Supply elasticity measures how quantity supplied responds to price. Important determinants include:

- production lead time;
- spare capacity;
- inventory possibilities;
- access to inputs;
- time available to adjust.

Supply is often more elastic over a longer horizon because firms have more time to change equipment, staffing, and supply chains.

## 8. Elasticity and Tax Incidence

The economic burden of a tax does not depend simply on who is legally required to pay it. The less elastic side of the market usually bears more of the burden because it has less ability to avoid the tax by changing quantity.

## 1. What Is Behavioral Economics?
- It combines psychology and economics to study how people's behavior systematically departs from the traditional economic assumption of the **rational person**.

## 2. Behavioral-Economics Assumptions

### 2.1. Irrationality
| Neoclassical rational-person assumption (narrow sense) | Behavioral-economics assumption (behavioral biases) |
| --- | --- |
| Cares only about one's absolute payoff | Reference-point comparison and loss aversion, social comparison |
| Plans and actions are consistent | Actions deviate from plans (self-control problems, overconsumption, procrastination) |
| Cares only about one's own interest | Complex social preferences (altruism, fairness, trust, reciprocity, retaliation, guilt, honesty, etc.) |
| Judges probabilistic events accurately | Probability-judgment biases (gambler's fallacy, hot-hand fallacy, overconfidence, etc.) |

## 3. Reference Points and Loss Aversion

### 3.1. Prospect Theory
#### 3.1.1. What Prospect Theory Is
When facing gains, people tend to avoid risk; when facing losses, people may become more willing to take risk.
The pain of a loss is felt more strongly than the pleasure of an equivalent gain, and gains and losses are evaluated relative to a reference point. Changing the reference point can change attitudes toward risk.

##### 3.1.1.1. Certainty Effect
Between a certain gain and "taking a gamble," many people choose the certain benefit.

##### 3.1.1.2. Reflection Effect
Between a certain loss and "taking a gamble," many people choose to gamble. This is called the reflection effect.

##### 3.1.1.3. Loss Aversion
The pleasure of unexpectedly receiving 100 yuan may not offset the pain of losing 100 yuan. This is called loss aversion.
Many people buy lottery tickets even when the probability of winning is tiny, still hoping for a small-probability outcome. This is the attraction to small-probability events described in the note.

##### 3.1.1.4. Reference Dependence
People often judge gains and losses relative to a reference point.
For example, given a choice between "others earn 60,000 yuan a year and you earn 70,000" and "others earn 90,000 and you earn 80,000," many people choose the former. This is reference dependence.

#### 3.1.2. Limitation of Prospect Theory
It describes how people behave, but does not by itself tell people how they ought to behave.

## 4. Actions Deviating from Plans
### 4.1. Present Bias
#### 4.1.1. Time Preference
- Decisions involving today and the future are called intertemporal choices. People have time preferences: future consumption may provide less current utility than present consumption.
- For example, with the same apple, eating it today may feel better than eating it tomorrow.

#### 4.1.2. What It Is
- If one can receive 1,000 yuan today or obtain 2% more in half a year, people may prefer receiving it today.
- If one can receive 1,000 yuan in half a year or obtain 2% more one year from now, people may prefer waiting until the later date.
- This illustrates present bias: in intertemporal decisions people may overweight immediate enjoyment, making choices involving the present especially impatient.

## 5. Social Preferences
### 5.1. What They Are
- Mainstream economics often models people as self-interested and focused on their own payoff.
- In many situations people care about more than their own payoff and show complex social preferences.

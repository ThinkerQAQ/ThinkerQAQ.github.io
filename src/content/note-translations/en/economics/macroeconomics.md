## 1. What Macroeconomics Studies

Macroeconomics focuses on economy-wide aggregates and relationships, including:

- total output and economic growth;
- employment and unemployment;
- inflation and the price level;
- aggregate consumption and investment;
- interest rates, money, and credit;
- fiscal and monetary policy;
- international trade and capital flows.

## 2. Long Run and Short Run

Macroeconomic analysis often separates two time horizons.

### Long run

Long-run analysis emphasizes productive capacity, including:

- labor and demographics;
- capital accumulation;
- human capital;
- technological progress;
- institutions and productivity.

### Short run

Short-run analysis emphasizes fluctuations of actual output around potential output and how demand, prices, financial conditions, and policy affect economic activity.

## 3. Accounting Identities and Behavioral Relationships

A useful distinction is between:

- **accounting identities**, which hold by definition, such as expenditure GDP `Y = C + I + G + NX`;
- **behavioral relationships**, which require a model and evidence, such as how consumption responds to income or investment responds to interest rates.

Variables appearing in the same accounting identity do not automatically have a causal relationship.

## 4. Schools of Thought and Models

The historical note reduced classical, Keynesian, and neoclassical traditions to “market school” versus “government school.” That is too coarse.

A more useful distinction is that different traditions make different assumptions about price and wage adjustment, expectations, market frictions, and policy effectiveness.

Common macro models include:

- income-determination models;
- IS-LM;
- AD-AS;
- growth models;
- modern dynamic models with sticky prices, expectations, and financial frictions.

They answer different questions and should not be treated as competing versions of a single universal truth.

## 5. Policy Objectives

Common macroeconomic policy objectives include:

- price stability;
- high and sustainable employment;
- stable long-run growth;
- financial stability;
- sustainable external balances in an open economy.

Trade-offs can arise, especially in the short run, so policy analysis should state the objective, time horizon, and side effects.

## 6. Reading Macro Data

At minimum, distinguish:

1. nominal versus real;
2. aggregate versus per-capita;
3. levels versus growth rates;
4. year-over-year versus period-over-period changes;
5. seasonally adjusted versus unadjusted data;
6. correlation versus causation.

A single indicator rarely describes the whole economy.

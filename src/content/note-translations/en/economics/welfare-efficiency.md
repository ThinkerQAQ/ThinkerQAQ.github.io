## 1. Efficiency and Fairness Are Different Questions

Economic efficiency often asks whether resources can be reallocated so that at least one person becomes better off without making anyone else worse off.

Fairness asks how resources and opportunities are distributed and what distributions society considers acceptable.

An outcome can be efficient but unfair, or more equal while involving an efficiency cost. Technical efficiency conditions therefore do not, by themselves, determine social welfare.

## 2. Pareto Improvement

A change is a **Pareto improvement** if it:

- makes at least one person better off; and
- makes no one else worse off.

An allocation is **Pareto efficient** when no further Pareto improvement is available.

Pareto efficiency says that no costless improvement remains. It does not say that the allocation is fair or uniquely desirable.

## 3. Consumer and Producer Surplus

In the basic competitive-market model:

- consumer surplus is the difference between willingness to pay and the amount actually paid;
- producer surplus is the difference between the amount received and the minimum amount sellers would accept.

Their sum is often used as a simple measure of total market surplus.

## 4. Taxes and Deadweight Loss

A tax creates a wedge between buyer and seller prices and may eliminate trades that would otherwise create gains from exchange. The lost surplus is called deadweight loss.

Its size depends partly on supply and demand elasticities: the more quantities respond to the tax, the larger the potential efficiency loss.

Taxes can also finance public services, redistribute income, or correct externalities, so the presence of deadweight loss alone does not determine whether a tax is desirable.

## 5. Market Failure

The efficiency result for competitive markets depends on strong assumptions. Common market failures include:

- externalities;
- public goods;
- market power;
- asymmetric information;
- some coordination failures.

These cases explain why policy analysis must examine institutions, information, and incentives in addition to prices and quantities.

## 6. Normative Judgments

Moving from “a policy increases total surplus” to “the policy is better” requires additional value judgments because policies usually create winners and losers.

A clear welfare analysis should therefore separate:

1. positive analysis of efficiency effects;
2. description of distributional effects;
3. normative judgments about fairness and policy goals.

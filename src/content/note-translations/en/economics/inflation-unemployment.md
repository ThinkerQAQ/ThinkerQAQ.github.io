## 1. What Inflation Is

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of a broad basket of goods and services, commonly measured through changes in price indexes such as CPI or PCE.

A price increase for one product is not, by itself, macroeconomic inflation. Likewise, a high price level is not the same thing as a high inflation rate: inflation describes the rate of change in prices.

## 2. Sources of Inflation

Inflation can emerge through several mechanisms:

- aggregate demand persistently exceeding sustainable productive capacity;
- energy, commodity, wage, or supply-chain cost shocks;
- exchange-rate changes affecting import prices;
- inflation expectations influencing wages and pricing;
- interactions among fiscal policy, monetary policy, and financial conditions.

The historical note used the exchange equation `MV = PY` to imply that more money mechanically produces proportional inflation. The equation is an identity, but causal conclusions require additional assumptions about velocity, real output, and money demand.

Money growth and inflation therefore should not be treated as a fixed one-for-one relationship.

## 3. Deflation

Deflation is a sustained decline in the general price level. It can accompany weak demand, rising real debt burdens, and economic weakness, but falling prices can also result from productivity growth or specific supply improvements.

The cause must be evaluated together with output, employment, wages, and financial conditions.

## 4. Measuring Unemployment

Labor-market statistics distinguish among:

- employed people;
- unemployed people;
- people outside the labor force.

A standard official definition generally requires an unemployed person to have no job, be available for work, and have actively looked for work recently.

The unemployment rate is usually:

`Unemployment Rate = Unemployed / Labor Force`

So not everyone without a job is counted as unemployed.

## 5. Frictional and Structural Unemployment

- **Frictional unemployment** comes from the time needed to search and match between jobs.
- **Structural unemployment** arises when workers' skills, locations, or industries do not match available jobs.

Both can exist even in a healthy economy, so full employment does not mean zero unemployment.

## 6. Cyclical Unemployment

When aggregate demand is weak and output falls below potential, firms may reduce production and employment, creating cyclical unemployment.

This component often falls as the economy recovers, although the speed depends on industry structure, skill matching, and labor-force participation.

## 7. Inflation and Unemployment

In the short run, demand changes can sometimes produce a trade-off between inflation and unemployment. But the relationship is not fixed. Supply shocks, inflation expectations, and institutional changes can cause inflation and unemployment to move in the same direction.

“Low unemployment always causes high inflation” is therefore not a stable universal rule.

## 1. What Monetary Policy Is

Monetary policy is the set of actions a central bank uses to influence policy rates, base money, banking-system liquidity, and financial conditions in pursuit of price stability, employment, or other statutory macroeconomic goals.

Modern monetary policy should not be reduced to “the central bank chooses M, and M determines the economy.” Policy often works first through short-term interest rates and broader financial conditions, then through lending, bond markets, exchange rates, asset prices, and expectations to influence consumption, investment, employment, and inflation.

## 2. Policy Rates and Transmission

In an interest-rate framework, a simplified transmission chain is:

`Policy rate → money-market rates → loan/bond/deposit rates → financial conditions → consumption and investment → output, employment, and inflation`

The process has lags and depends on bank balance sheets, risk appetite, household and firm expectations, and the financial structure of the economy.

## 3. Common Policy Tools

Central banks can use tools such as:

- open-market operations;
- standing lending facilities;
- reserve requirements;
- interest on reserve balances;
- asset purchases and other unconventional tools;
- targeted or structural liquidity and credit-support facilities.

Operating frameworks differ across economies and can change significantly over time.

## 4. Changes in China's Policy-Rate Framework

The historical note treated the MLF rate as a key medium-term policy rate and linked LPR directly to MLF. That framing is now outdated.

From 2024 onward, the People's Bank of China further clarified the **7-day reverse-repo operation rate** as its main policy rate and reduced the policy-signaling role of MLF. From March 2025, MLF moved to multiple-price allotment without a single winning rate, making it more clearly a medium-term liquidity-provision tool.

This is a good example of why old monetary-policy notes must always be read with a date attached.

## 5. LPR

The Loan Prime Rate is an important reference for bank loan pricing in China.

It is not a centrally imposed final lending rate for every borrower. Actual loan rates also depend on borrower risk, maturity, collateral, competition, and bank funding conditions.

## 6. M0, M1, and M2

Money-supply aggregates group financial instruments by liquidity. The exact statistical definitions vary by country and over time.

Starting with **January 2025** data, the PBOC revised China's M1 definition to include:

`M1 = M0 + corporate demand deposits + personal demand deposits + customer reserve funds held by non-bank payment institutions`

The historical note's simpler `M1 = M0 + demand deposits` no longer captures the current official Chinese definition accurately.

## 7. The Money Multiplier

Textbooks often write:

`M = m × B`

where `B` is the monetary base and `m` is the money multiplier.

This is useful for understanding how deposits and credit can expand through the banking system, but real-world money creation is not a mechanical process with a fixed multiplier controlled directly by the central bank. Lending demand, bank capital, regulation, reserve frameworks, and interest rates all matter.

## 8. Exchange Rates and Asset Prices

Rate increases and cuts affect capital flows, exchange rates, and asset valuations, but the direction and magnitude are not mechanically fixed.

Statements such as “rate hikes always make stocks fall” or “currency depreciation always makes exporters rise” ignore expectations, earnings, risk premia, and policy combinations.

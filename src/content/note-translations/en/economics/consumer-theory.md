## 1. Utility

Utility represents a consumer's preferences over consumption bundles. Modern microeconomics does not require utility to be interpreted as a directly measurable “happiness score”; the important role is to represent the ranking of preferences.

The historical note's total utility, marginal utility, and diminishing marginal utility are useful intuitive models, but current analysis more often works directly with preferences, budget constraints, and indifference curves.

## 2. Basic Preference Assumptions

Standard consumer theory often uses assumptions such as:

- **completeness**: the consumer can compare two bundles;
- **transitivity**: if A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to C;
- **local nonsatiation**: all else equal, a little more of a desirable good is generally preferred.

These are modeling assumptions, not claims that every real decision always follows them.

## 3. Indifference Curves

An indifference curve connects combinations of goods that give the consumer the same preference level.

Under common assumptions for desirable goods:

- curves farther to the northeast represent more preferred bundles;
- different indifference curves cannot intersect;
- preference for diversification often produces curves convex to the origin.

Perfect substitutes and perfect complements are common special cases.

## 4. The Budget Constraint

For two goods, a budget constraint can be written as:

`PₓX + PᵧY ≤ I`

where `I` is income and `Pₓ` and `Pᵧ` are prices.

- A change in income shifts the budget line.
- A change in one price changes the slope and intercept.
- Bundles outside the budget set are unaffordable at current prices.

## 5. Consumer Optimum

In the standard model, the consumer chooses the most preferred affordable bundle. For a smooth interior solution, a common condition is:

`MRS = Pₓ / Pᵧ`

The consumer's marginal willingness to substitute between goods matches the market relative price.

An optimum can also occur at a corner, so tangency is not a universal requirement.

## 6. Consumer Surplus

Consumer surplus is the difference between the maximum amount a consumer is willing to pay and the amount actually paid. At the market level, it is often represented as the area below the demand curve and above the market price.

It is a welfare-analysis tool, but it does not imply that more developed markets automatically create more consumer surplus.

## 7. Income and Substitution Effects

A price change creates two conceptually separate effects:

- **substitution effect**: relative prices change, encouraging substitution between goods;
- **income effect**: the price change alters real purchasing power.

For ordinary goods, these often work together to produce the usual inverse relationship between own price and quantity demanded. Inferior goods have a different income-effect direction, and the extreme case can produce a Giffen good.

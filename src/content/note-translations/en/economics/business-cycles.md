## 1. What a Business Cycle Is

Over the long run, an economy often grows around a trend. In the short run, actual output, employment, investment, and consumption fluctuate around that trend. These fluctuations are commonly called the business cycle.

Modern macroeconomics generally does not assume that the economy repeats on a fixed timetable. Labels such as 3–5 year or 45–60 year cycles are better treated as historical classifications than forecasting rules.

## 2. Potential Output and the Output Gap

Potential output is the level of production an economy can sustain with resources used at normal, sustainable rates.

The difference between actual and potential output helps describe cyclical conditions:

- actual output below potential: underused resources and greater unemployment pressure;
- actual output above a sustainable level: stronger overheating and inflation pressure may emerge.

Potential output is not directly observable, so estimates of the output gap are uncertain.

## 3. Cycle Phases

“Recession → recovery → expansion → overheating” is a useful vocabulary, but real cycles do not always move through each phase neatly.

Shocks can come from:

- changes in demand;
- supply disruptions;
- financial conditions;
- technology and productivity;
- fiscal or monetary policy;
- external trade or geopolitical events.

## 4. The Merrill Lynch Investment Clock

The historical note mapped cycle stages mechanically to asset choices. The investment clock is more useful as a framework for thinking about how **growth and inflation regimes** can affect asset behavior.

It should not be treated as a deterministic trading rule because:

- cycle regimes are often identified more clearly after the fact;
- asset prices incorporate expectations in advance;
- valuations, interest rates, and policy paths differ across cycles;
- countries have different institutions and financial structures;
- historical averages do not guarantee future repetition.

## 5. Observing the Cycle

Useful indicators include:

- real GDP and industrial production;
- employment, unemployment, and hours worked;
- consumption and investment;
- PMI and business surveys;
- inflation;
- credit and broader financial conditions;
- fiscal and monetary policy.

No single indicator is normally sufficient to identify the cycle reliably.

## 6. Cycles vs. Long-Run Growth

Cyclical fluctuations and long-run growth are different problems.

Short-run stabilization can influence aggregate demand and resource utilization. Long-run growth in output per person depends more fundamentally on productivity, human capital, capital accumulation, technology, and institutions.

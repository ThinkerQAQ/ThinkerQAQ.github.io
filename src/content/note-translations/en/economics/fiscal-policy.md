## 1. What Fiscal Policy Is

Fiscal policy is the use of **government spending, taxation, and related budget decisions** to influence economic activity.

The historical note defined fiscal policy as adjusting the money supply or credit. That is a monetary-policy function, so this edition corrects the definition.

## 2. Government Revenue

Taxes are a major source of government revenue. They can be classified in several ways, including:

- taxes on income, consumption, or property;
- direct and indirect taxes;
- progressive, proportional, or regressive structures.

Government borrowing is not a tax; it is a financing method that creates public debt.

## 3. Government Spending

Fiscal spending includes:

- government purchases of goods and services;
- public investment;
- transfers to households or firms;
- interest and other budget expenditures.

In expenditure GDP, `G` primarily represents government consumption and investment in final goods and services. Transfer payments are not themselves current production and therefore are not directly included as `G`, although recipients may later spend the income on consumption.

## 4. Expansion and Contraction

All else equal:

- higher government purchases, tax cuts, or larger transfers can raise aggregate demand and are commonly described as expansionary fiscal policy;
- lower spending or higher taxes can reduce aggregate demand and are commonly described as contractionary fiscal policy.

Actual effects depend on the state of the economy, policy duration, financing, expectations, imports, and monetary-policy responses.

## 5. Fiscal Multipliers

The effect of fiscal policy on GDP need not equal the initial change in spending or taxes.

New spending becomes income for others and can generate further consumption or investment, while saving, imports, taxes, interest-rate changes, and capacity constraints can weaken the transmission.

The fiscal multiplier is therefore not a universal constant. It can differ across recessions and expansions, interest-rate environments, open and closed economies, and types of fiscal action.

## 6. Automatic Stabilizers

Some fiscal mechanisms change with the business cycle without new legislation. Examples include:

- progressive taxes that fall as income falls;
- unemployment benefits and similar transfers that rise when the economy weakens.

These mechanisms can damp fluctuations in disposable income and aggregate demand.

## 7. Fiscal Sustainability

Fiscal stimulus is not costless. Analysis should also consider:

- public debt and interest expense;
- the long-run return on spending;
- possible crowding out of private investment;
- future tax burdens;
- demographics and long-term commitments.

“Larger deficits stimulate the economy” is only the first layer of the analysis.

## 1. What Defines a Market Structure

Market structure is commonly analyzed through:

- the number and concentration of firms;
- whether products are homogeneous or differentiated;
- barriers to entry and exit;
- the degree of pricing power;
- whether firms must react strategically to one another.

The four classic models are perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly. Real markets usually approximate these models rather than fitting one perfectly.

## 2. Perfect Competition

The standard model assumes:

- many small buyers and sellers;
- homogeneous products;
- relatively free entry and exit;
- sufficiently available information.

An individual firm is a price taker, so:

`P = MR`

Profit maximization implies:

`P = MR = MC`

With free entry and exit in the standard long-run model, economic profit is pushed toward zero.

Calling a securities or agricultural market “perfectly competitive” should be treated as an approximation, not a strict classification.

## 3. Monopoly

A monopoly serves the market as a single firm and faces significant barriers to entry.

Because selling more output generally requires moving down the demand curve:

`MR < P`

The monopolist still chooses output where `MR = MC`, then reads the corresponding price from demand.

Sources of monopoly can include legal privileges, natural-monopoly cost structures, control of key resources, technology, network effects, or scale-related entry barriers.

## 4. Price Discrimination

Price discrimination means charging different prices for identical or very similar products when the price differences are not fully explained by cost differences.

Classic types are:

- first degree: prices tailored as closely as possible to willingness to pay;
- second degree: prices vary with quantity or package choice;
- third degree: different identifiable market segments face different prices.

It generally requires market power, an ability to segment buyers, and limits on resale between low-price and high-price markets.

## 5. Monopolistic Competition

Monopolistic competition typically combines:

- many firms;
- differentiated products;
- relatively easy entry and exit.

Differentiation gives each firm some pricing power, while entry limits long-run economic profit.

## 6. Oligopoly

In oligopoly, a small number of major firms interact strategically. Common models include:

- Cournot: firms choose quantities;
- Bertrand: firms choose prices;
- Stackelberg: firms move sequentially;
- repeated games: current actions affect future interaction.

The historical note's kinked-demand and price-leadership models are useful special cases, but they do not characterize every oligopoly.

## 7. Prisoner's Dilemma

The prisoner's dilemma shows how individually rational strategies can produce an outcome that leaves all players worse off than a coordinated alternative.

It helps explain price wars, advertising races, and other strategic problems. Whether cooperation can persist depends on repeated interaction, information, punishment mechanisms, and regulation.

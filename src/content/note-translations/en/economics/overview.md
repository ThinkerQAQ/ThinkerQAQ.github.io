## 1. What Economics Studies

Economics studies how individuals, firms, and societies make choices when resources are limited and have competing uses, and how those choices interact through institutions and markets.

Three useful core ideas are:

- **scarcity**: resources cannot satisfy every possible use at the same time;
- **choice**: trade-offs are unavoidable;
- **opportunity cost**: choosing one option means giving up the most valuable alternative.

The historical note described economics as making choices under limited resources. That is a sound starting point, but assumptions such as perfect rationality and complete information should be treated as simplifying assumptions in particular models, not as facts about the real world.

## 2. Positive and Normative Analysis

Economic questions can be separated into two broad types:

- **positive analysis** asks what is happening and how variables are related;
- **normative analysis** asks what should happen and therefore contains value judgments.

Keeping them separate helps prevent a model result from being presented as a moral or policy conclusion by itself.

## 3. Microeconomics

Microeconomics studies individual decisions and specific markets, including:

- consumer choice under budget constraints;
- how firms combine labor, capital, and other inputs;
- how supply and demand determine prices and quantities;
- the differences among competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly;
- how taxes, subsidies, and price controls change market outcomes.

## 4. Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics studies economy-wide aggregates and relationships, including:

- GDP and long-run economic growth;
- business cycles;
- inflation and unemployment;
- fiscal policy;
- monetary policy.

Micro and macroeconomics are connected. Individual household and firm decisions aggregate into consumption, investment, employment, and price outcomes at the macro level.

## 5. Behavioral Economics

Standard models often simplify decision-makers as stable, rational, and internally consistent. Behavioral economics introduces psychological evidence and studies systematic patterns such as loss aversion, reference dependence, present bias, and overconfidence.

Its point is not simply that “people are irrational,” but to identify situations where real decisions predictably differ from standard models.

## 6. How to Use Economic Models

An economic model is a compressed representation of reality. When using one, ask:

1. What assumptions does it make?
2. What variables does it leave out?
3. Under what conditions does its conclusion hold?
4. Does the data support the proposed mechanism?

Supply-demand diagrams, utility maximization, macro models, and behavioral biases are analytical tools rather than universal explanations.

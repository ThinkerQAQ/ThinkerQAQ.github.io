## 1. What Behavioral Economics Studies

Behavioral economics combines economics and psychology to study systematic ways in which real decisions differ from standard economic models and how those differences affect markets and policy.

The goal is not simply to label people “irrational,” but to identify **repeatable and predictable behavioral patterns**.

## 2. Prospect Theory

Prospect theory emphasizes that people often evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point rather than only through final wealth.

Typical patterns include:

- **reference dependence**: the same outcome can feel different under different reference points;
- **loss aversion**: losses of a given size often have a stronger psychological effect than equal-sized gains;
- **state-dependent risk attitudes**: risk preferences can differ across gain and loss domains;
- **probability weighting**: people may overweight some small-probability events in decision weights.

These are empirical patterns, not claims that every individual in every setting has the same behavioral parameters.

## 3. Present Bias

Standard exponential discounting assumes relatively consistent intertemporal preferences. In practice, people often place extra weight on the present.

Possible consequences include:

- procrastination;
- undersaving;
- difficulty executing plans that are beneficial in the long run;
- inconsistency between intentions and actions.

Commitment devices, automatic saving, and default options can reduce these problems, although good policy design must also preserve transparency and the ability to opt out.

## 4. Social Preferences

People do not always care only about their own absolute payoff. Experimental economics often observes:

- fairness preferences;
- altruism;
- reciprocity;
- trust;
- concern for social image.

Dictator games, ultimatum games, and trust games are classic experimental tools for studying these behaviors.

These experiments show that pure self-interest is not a complete description of all behavior, while also requiring caution when generalizing from experimental settings to real institutions.

## 5. Probability-Judgment Biases

Probability judgments can systematically differ from Bayesian updating or statistical reasoning. Examples include:

- base-rate neglect;
- extrapolation from small samples;
- the gambler's fallacy;
- overconfidence;
- projection bias.

The “hot hand” requires more nuance than the historical note gave it. Early research often treated belief in streaks as a bias, while later work has found measurable hot-hand effects in some real skill settings. Not every expectation of continued success after a streak is therefore a fallacy.

## 6. Behavioral Biases in Investing

Behavioral economics can help explain patterns such as:

- loss aversion delaying realization of losses;
- overconfidence leading to excessive trading;
- extrapolating recent returns too far into the future;
- anchoring on historical prices;
- herd behavior amplifying market moves.

These mechanisms can explain behavior, but they do not automatically generate reliable trading strategies.

## 7. Behavioral Policy

Behavioral policy often changes choice architecture through:

- default options;
- framing and information presentation;
- reminders;
- commitment mechanisms;
- process simplification.

Evaluation should consider not only effectiveness but also transparency, autonomy, opt-out costs, and distributional effects.

## 8. Relationship to Standard Economics

Behavioral economics does not discard standard economics. It extends the toolkit by:

1. identifying settings where standard models predict poorly;
2. introducing more realistic psychological mechanisms;
3. testing whether those mechanisms improve explanation and prediction.

It is best understood as an extension of the economic modeling toolkit.
